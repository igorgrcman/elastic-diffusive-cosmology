## Abstract

We derive the Jeans mass criterion for gravitational collapse from the five-dimensional geometry of Elastic Diffusive Cosmology (EDC). In this framework, gravity emerges from membrane curvature in the 5D Bulk, while electromagnetic forces arise from flux tubes connecting topological defects. The vast hierarchy between gravitational and electromagnetic coupling constants ($\alpha_G/\alpha \sim 10^{-36}$) emerges naturally from the geometric structure, explaining why gravity dominates only at astronomical scales despite being intrinsically weaker. We show that star formation occurs when accumulated membrane curvature from neutral matter exceeds thermal motion, causing runaway collapse into deep "wells" in the 5D geometry.

**Keywords:** Jeans mass, gravitational collapse, star formation, extra dimensions, membrane theory, EDC

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Hierarchy Problem in Gravity

One of the deepest mysteries in physics is the enormous disparity between the electromagnetic and gravitational forces:

$$\frac{F_{EM}}{F_G} = \frac{e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)}{Gm_p^2} \approx 10^{36}$$

Why is gravity so weak? And if gravity is so weak, how does it dominate the large-scale structure of the universe—collapsing gas clouds into stars, stars into galaxies, and shaping cosmic evolution?

### 1.2 The Jeans Criterion

In 1902, James Jeans derived the criterion for gravitational instability in a gas cloud. A cloud of mass M and radius R collapses when gravitational potential energy exceeds thermal kinetic energy:

$$|E_g| > E_K$$

$$\frac{GM^2}{R} > Nk_BT$$

This defines the **Jeans mass** $M_J$—the critical mass above which collapse occurs.

### 1.3 The EDC Approach

Elastic Diffusive Cosmology provides a geometric origin for both gravity and electromagnetism:

- **Gravity:** Curvature of the 3D membrane in 5D Bulk
- **Electromagnetism:** Flux tubes connecting topological defects

In this paper, we derive the Jeans mass from EDC first principles, showing how the geometric structure naturally explains the hierarchy between forces and the transition from atomic to astronomical physics.

## 4. Energy Balance for Collapse

### 4.1 Gravitational Potential Energy

For a uniform sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$:

$$E_g = -\frac{3GM^2}{5R}$$

In EDC terms:

$$E_g = -\frac{3\alpha_G \sigma_{eff} r_e^3}{5m_p^2} \cdot \frac{M^2}{R}$$

### 4.2 Thermal Kinetic Energy

For $N$ particles at temperature $T$:

$$E_K = \frac{3}{2}Nk_BT = \frac{3}{2}\frac{M}{m_H}k_BT$$

### 4.3 Collapse Criterion

Collapse occurs when:

$$|E_g| > E_K$$

$$\frac{3GM^2}{5R} > \frac{3Mk_BT}{2m_H}$$

$$\frac{GM}{R} > \frac{5k_BT}{2m_H}$$

## 5. Derivation of Jeans Mass

### 5.1 Jeans Radius

For a cloud of density $\rho$:

$$M = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho$$

Substituting into the collapse criterion:

$$\frac{G \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho}{R} > \frac{5k_BT}{2m_H}$$

$$R^2 > \frac{15k_BT}{8\pi G\rho m_H}$$

**Jeans radius:**

$$\boxed{R_J = \sqrt{\frac{15k_BT}{8\pi G\rho m_H}}}$$

### 5.2 Jeans Mass

$$M_J = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_J^3 \rho$$

$$\boxed{M_J = \left(\frac{5k_BT}{2Gm_H}\right)^{3/2} \left(\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\right)^{1/2}}$$

### 5.3 Expression in EDC Parameters

Substituting $G = \alpha_G \sigma_{eff} r_e^3 / m_p^2$:

$$M_J = \left(\frac{5k_BT \cdot m_p^2}{2\alpha_G \sigma_{eff} r_e^3 \cdot m_H}\right)^{3/2} \left(\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\right)^{1/2}$$

With $m_H \approx m_p$:

$$\boxed{M_J = \frac{1}{\alpha_G^{3/2}} \left(\frac{5k_BT}{2\sigma_{eff} r_e^3 / m_p}\right)^{3/2} \left(\frac{3}{4\pi\rho}\right)^{1/2}}$$

### 5.4 Characteristic Temperature Scale

Define the **gravitational temperature**:

$$T_G \equiv \frac{\alpha_G \sigma_{eff} r_e^3}{k_B m_p}$$

Numerically:

$$T_G = \frac{(5.9 \times 10^{-39})(1.41 \times 10^{18})(2.24 \times 10^{-44})}{(1.38 \times 10^{-23})(1.67 \times 10^{-27})}$$

$$T_G = \frac{1.86 \times 10^{-64}}{2.30 \times 10^{-50}} = 8.1 \times 10^{-15} \text{ K}$$

This extraordinarily small temperature reflects the weakness of gravity.

### 5.5 Jeans Mass in Terms of $T_G$

$$M_J = \left(\frac{T}{T_G}\right)^{3/2} \cdot m_p \cdot f(\rho)$$

where $f(\rho)$ is a density-dependent factor.

For a typical molecular cloud ($T = 10$ K):

$$\frac{T}{T_G} = \frac{10}{8 \times 10^{-15}} \approx 1.25 \times 10^{15}$$

This large ratio explains why macroscopic masses (not individual particles) are needed for gravitational collapse.

$$\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma_{eff}}{R_{min}}$$
