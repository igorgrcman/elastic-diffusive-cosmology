\section{Introduction: The Stability Problem}
\label{sec:frozen_intro}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Chapter~\ref{ch:ontology} established that particles are 5D topological
structures. But this raises an immediate question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{If particles are dynamic 5D objects, why do we observe them as
stable, static 3D entities with definite masses and charges?}
\end{quote}

The answer is the \textbf{frozen regime}---a dynamical condition where
5D evolution is effectively halted on observable timescales.

\begin{statusbox}{The Frozen Regime}
\textbf{Definition} \tagP{}: The frozen regime is the condition where:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Topology-changing transitions are exponentially suppressed
\item Internal structure is ``locked'' relative to measurement timescales
\item 3D projection gives well-defined, stable observables
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Physical picture}: The membrane is like a frozen lake---locally
solid and stable, though fundamentally still water (dynamic 5D field).
\end{statusbox}

\section{Timescale Separation}
\label{sec:timescale_separation}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The frozen regime emerges from a separation of timescales:

\subsection{The Hierarchy}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llc}
\toprule
\textbf{Process} & \textbf{Timescale} & \textbf{Regime} \\
\midrule
Internal oscillations & $\tau_{\text{int}} \sim 10^{-23}$ s & Fast \\
Strong interactions & $\tau_{\text{strong}} \sim 10^{-23}$ s & Fast \\
EM transitions & $\tau_{\text{EM}} \sim 10^{-16}$ s & Intermediate \\
Weak decays & $\tau_{\text{weak}} \sim 10^{-10}$ to $10^3$ s & Slow \\
Observation & $\tau_{\text{obs}} \gg 10^{-23}$ s & Much slower \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Key observation}: For any process where $\tau_{\text{int}} \ll \tau_{\text{obs}}$,
internal structure appears ``averaged out'' to the observer.

\subsection{Adiabatic Limit}

When internal dynamics are much faster than observation:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\tau_{\text{int}}}{\tau_{\text{obs}}} \ll 1 \quad \implies \quad \text{Adiabatic limit}
\end{equation}

In this limit \tagDc{}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Observer sees time-averaged (expectation value) quantities
\item Internal fluctuations average to zero
\item Effective 3D description becomes valid
\end{itemize}

\section{The Frozen Criterion}
\label{sec:frozen_criterion}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Two complementary derivations establish the frozen criterion---both
from the 5D action (Paper 2):

\subsection{Route A: Large-$\sigma$ Instanton Barrier}

\begin{mechanism}{Frozen via Instanton Suppression}
\textbf{Setup} \tagDc{}:
Topology-changing transitions (e.g., vortex unwinding) require
passing through a configuration barrier.

\textbf{Tunneling rate}:
\begin{equation}
\Gamma \sim \Gamma_0 \exp\left(-\frac{\sigma \cdot \Delta A}{\hbar}\right)
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item $\sigma$: Membrane tension (MeV/fm$^2$)
\item $\Delta A$: Change in membrane area for transition
\item $\Gamma_0$: Attempt frequency
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Frozen criterion}:
\begin{equation}
\sigma \cdot \Delta A_{\min} > \hbar \ln(\Gamma_0 \cdot \tau_{\text{obs}}) \quad \tagDcSym
\end{equation}

\textbf{Physical meaning}: If membrane tension is high enough, the
``energy cost'' of topology change exceeds the available thermal/quantum
fluctuations over any reasonable observation time.
\end{mechanism}

\subsection{Route B: Topological Superselection}

\begin{mechanism}{Frozen via Superselection}
\textbf{Setup} \tagP{}:
Winding numbers are topological invariants.

\textbf{Argument}:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item[(B1)] Winding numbers are integers: $W \in \mathbb{Z}$ \tagM
\item[(B2)] No topology-changing process during $\tau_{\text{obs}}$ \tagP
\item[(B3)] Therefore: $\Gamma = 0$ (exactly, not approximately) \tagDc
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Physical meaning}: If we POSTULATE that topology cannot change
(superselection), then the frozen regime is exact, not approximate.
\end{mechanism}

\subsection{Comparison}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Route} & \textbf{Character} & \textbf{Epistemic Status} \\
\midrule
A (Instanton) & Exponential suppression & \tagDc{} (needs $\sigma$ value) \\
B (Superselection) & Exact zero & \tagP{} (postulate-dependent) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Both routes give the same practical conclusion: topological structure is stable.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]

\item \textbf{Conserved charges}: Electric charge, color charge, baryon number
\item \textbf{Spin structure}: $S^3 \to S^2$ projection (half-integer vs integer)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What Remains Dynamic}

Even in the frozen regime, some processes are allowed:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Phase oscillations}: Internal $U(1)$ phase can evolve
\item \textbf{Vibrational modes}: Junction can vibrate around equilibrium
\item \textbf{Interactions}: External fields can couple to frozen structure
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Step Function Limit}

For computational purposes, the frozen profile can be modeled as:
\begin{equation}
f(r) = \Theta(r - a) = \begin{cases} 0 & r < a \\ 1 & r \geq a \end{cases}
\quad \tagDcApprox
\end{equation}
This step function is the \textit{asymptotic form} of the frozen profile
in the large-$\sigma$ limit---an approximation choice, not a derivation.

Given this step-function model, the geometric factor evaluates exactly \tagDerSym{}:
\begin{equation}
C = \frac{4\pi}{3} \quad \text{(parameter-free within the step-function limit)}
\end{equation}

\section{Causality Direction}
\label{sec:causality}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A crucial conceptual point:

\begin{warningbox}{Causal Direction is 5D $\to$ 3D (One-Way)}
The relationship between 5D and 3D is \textbf{not symmetric}:

\textbf{Forward (valid)}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item 5D geometry $\to$ 3D mass
\item Junction configuration $\to$ Proton properties
\item Membrane tension $\to$ Nuclear scales
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Backward (invalid)}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item 3D measurements $\not\to$ Cannot ``change'' 5D
\item Observing mass $\not\to$ Doesn't affect geometry
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Physical analogy}: Shadows on a wall are caused by 3D objects
in the light. Looking at shadows doesn't change the objects.
\end{warningbox}

This has profound implications for the nature of fundamental constants
(see Epilogue, Chapter~\ref{ch:beyond}).
